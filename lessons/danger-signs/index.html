Danger Signs After Birth

Most mothers recover well after birth. But sometimes serious problems appear in the hours, days, or weeks afterward — and knowing the warning signs, and acting on them quickly, can save a mother's life.

This lesson helps you recognise the signs that mean a mother needs care right away, and what to do when you see them.

⏱ 18–25 min 🎯 Community health workers & families 📋 6 sections, checks along the way

By the end, you'll be able to

  • ✓ Recognise the full range of physical and mental health danger signs after birth, including breathing difficulty
  • ✓ Know that these signs mean "go now" — not "wait and see"
  • ✓ Take the right first-aid action while getting her to care
  • ✓ Recognise when a mother's feelings, not just her body, need attention too

Key point

None of these signs need to be confirmed by a health worker before you act. If you see one, get her to care — don't wait.

Section 1 · Bleeding

Heavy bleeding

A little bleeding after birth is normal and can continue on and off for a few weeks. It becomes a danger sign when it's heavy — soaking through a cloth or pad in under an hour, passing large clots, or bleeding that suddenly gets worse instead of settling down.

Heavy bleeding can happen right after birth, or it can start again days later — both need care right away.

Watch for

Soaking through a cloth in under an hour, large clots, or feeling dizzy/faint along with the bleeding — get her to a health facility immediately.

Key point

If she feels dizzy, faint, or her pulse feels fast and weak, treat it as an emergency even if the bleeding itself doesn't look heavy yet.

Quick check

Which of these is a danger sign, not normal bleeding?

Correct: soaking a cloth in under an hour, large clots, or bleeding that suddenly worsens all mean she needs care now.

Section 1 · Bleeding

While you get her to care

Help her lie down and raise her legs if you can. This helps keep blood flowing to her heart and brain while you arrange transport to a health facility.

Quick check

While getting her to care for heavy bleeding, you should:

Correct: help her lie down and raise her legs if you can — this helps keep blood flowing to her heart and brain while you get her to care.

Section 2 · Convulsions

Fits, severe headache, or blurred vision

A convulsion (fitting or shaking that the mother can't control) after birth is a medical emergency. It's often linked to dangerously high blood pressure.

A severe headache that won't go away, blurred or dimmed vision, or swelling in the face and hands can come before a convulsion — these are warning signs on their own, even without a fit yet. Health workers call this pre-eclampsia, or eclampsia once a fit happens.

Quick check

A severe headache with blurred vision after birth is:

Correct: severe headache, blurred vision, or swelling can appear before a convulsion — treat them as urgent on their own.

Section 2 · Convulsions

If a convulsion happens

Keep her safe from injury, turn her onto her side if you can, and get her to a health facility as fast as possible.

Watch for

Don't put anything in her mouth or try to hold her still — just keep her safe from injury and turn her on her side.

Quick check

If a mother has a convulsion, you should:

Correct: never put anything in her mouth or try to hold her still — this can cause injury. Keep her safe, turn her on her side, and get her to a health facility fast.

Section 3 · Fever & pain

Fever, severe pain, or bad-smelling discharge

A fever (feeling hot to the touch, sweaty, or shivery, and not improving) along with feeling very unwell can be a sign of infection. This can happen in the days or weeks after birth, not just right away.

Severe pain in the belly, or vaginal discharge that smells bad, are also signs of possible infection — especially together with fever.

Pain, redness, swelling, or fluid coming from a stitch or wound (from a tear or a caesarean) is a sign that it may be infected and needs checking. For a caesarean wound, also watch for the edges opening or pulling apart — this needs care right away, not just when it looks infected.

Quick check

Fever plus bad-smelling discharge after birth most likely points to:

Correct: fever with bad-smelling discharge or severe belly pain points to possible infection — she needs to be seen.

Section 4 · Breathing

Fast or difficult breathing, or chest pain

Breathing that's fast, difficult, or feels like she can't get enough air is a danger sign — as is chest pain. This can happen even without heavy bleeding.

It's easy to mistake this for tiredness after birth. If breathing trouble or chest pain doesn't ease with rest, treat it as urgent.

If you can, help her sit up rather than lie flat while you get her to care — this can make breathing a little easier.

Watch for

Fast or laboured breathing, chest pain, or feeling unable to catch her breath — get her to a health facility right away, don't wait to see if it passes.

Quick check

Fast or difficult breathing after birth that doesn't ease with rest is:

Correct: breathing trouble or chest pain that doesn't ease with rest is a danger sign on its own — get her to care right away.

Section 5 · How she's feeling

Her feelings matter too

This part can be harder to talk about — but it matters just as much as the physical signs.

Feeling tired, tearful, or overwhelmed in the first days after birth is common and usually passes. But some mothers experience something more serious.

These are danger signs too: feeling very sad or hopeless for more than a couple of weeks, not being able to cope with daily life or caring for the baby, or having thoughts of harming herself or the baby.

A mother who has gone quiet, withdrawn, or stopped wanting to hold or feed her baby is showing signs worth taking seriously — this isn't something to wait out.

If she seems confused, is seeing or hearing things that aren't there, or is acting very differently than usual, this also needs care right away — even without sadness or hopelessness.

If she has thoughts of harming herself or the baby, don't leave her alone — stay with her and get her to care right away, the same as any other danger sign.

Key point

Family and friends are often the first to notice a change in how a mother seems — you don't need to be a health worker to recognise this and encourage her to get help.

Quick check

Which of these should be treated as a danger sign?

Correct: thoughts of self-harm or harming the baby, or ongoing hopelessness and inability to cope, need care right away, just like a physical danger sign.

Section 6 · What to do

Act fast — don't wait and see

Don't wait for the sign to get worse, and don't wait for it to be confirmed. If you see any of these signs, get the mother to the nearest health facility as quickly as you can.

Don't travel alone if you can help it. Take someone with her, and take any notes on what you've seen and when it started — this helps the health worker who sees her.

If it's happened before, or the family already has a plan for how to get to care quickly (transport, who to call), use it — this is exactly the situation it's for.

Watch for

Delay is the biggest danger. Most mothers who die from these problems die because care came too late — not because the problem couldn't be treated.

Quick check

Tick all the steps you should take when you see a danger sign:

Correct: get her to care right away, take someone with her, and note what you saw and when it started. Don't wait to see if it gets worse, and don't wait for confirmation — act as soon as you see the sign.
